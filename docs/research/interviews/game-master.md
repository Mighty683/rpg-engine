# Simulated interview: game master

**Date:** 2026-11-04  
**Method:** The main agent asked one question per exchange; a separate AI subagent answered only as the game master. Follow-ups were chosen after receiving each answer. The [provisional persona](../personas/game-master.md) was a loose guide, not an answer script. This transcript replaces the earlier over-constrained interview; the subsequent draft in which the subagent wrote both questions and answers was not used as the interview.  
**Status:** Simulated; not a participant interview. Events, preferences, and quotations below are fictional, not participant evidence or validated requirements.

## Fictional setup

The subagent chose Marek, a game master running a monthly fantasy campaign for four friends, with a laptop, paper notes, physical dice, a messaging group, and occasional virtual-tabletop use for remote participation. This setup was retained from its exploratory draft for continuity. Marek has not tested RPG Engine. These invented details do not update or validate the source persona.

## Interview

### 1. Where management interrupted play

**Interviewer:** Think about the last session in your fictional campaign. At what moment did managing the game get in the way of actually running it? Walk me through what happened.

**Marek:** The fight in the archive was the worst moment. One character had been injured before it started, another had a temporary effect running, and the guard captain had promised them a favor just before things went bad. I was trying to keep all of that straight while also describing what the guards were doing.

I had turn order scribbled on paper, the character summary on my laptop, and the captain’s notes in another document. A player asked whether their effect was still active, and I realized I hadn’t marked which turn it started on. I stopped the scene, flipped through my notes, and asked the group what they remembered. We agreed on an answer, but by then everyone had drifted into a side conversation.

What bothered me wasn’t getting a rule wrong. It was that I’d gone from reacting to the players to acting like a clerk. When we picked up again, I had to remind myself what the captain was about to do.

### 2. Why keep multiple tools?

**Interviewer:** You said you felt like a clerk, but you also had information split across paper and two documents. Why had you arranged it that way, and which parts of that setup would you want to keep?

**Marek:** It grew that way because each piece solved a different problem. Turn order is on paper because I can cross things out and add a guard without clicking around. The character summaries live on my laptop because I update them between sessions and can search them. The captain’s notes are in my adventure document because I wrote her as part of that location, not as someone I expected to track during a fight.

I’d keep the paper for quick marks and sketches. I’d also keep preparing fuller notes on the laptop; writing them helps me understand the situation, even if I only need a few lines at the table.

What I’d change is the distance between those pieces. Once the captain became important, I wanted her promise and immediate details beside the fight notes, not buried in the original document. I don’t necessarily need everything in one place permanently. I need to bring the relevant bits together when the situation changes, without spending five minutes reorganizing them while everyone waits.

### 3. Capturing an unexpected development

**Interviewer:** How did you handle the captain's unexpected importance during play and after the session? What did you write down, what did you leave out, and how would you find it next month?

**Marek:** During play I wrote “captain owes favor” in the margin of my turn-order sheet, with her name and a question mark beside it. I didn’t stop to write out the conversation. I also jotted down that she’d seen the group enter the archive, because I thought that might matter later.

After everyone left, I copied the promise into my session notes and added a couple of sentences about how it came about. I put a reference to it in the captain’s existing notes too, so I’d see it when preparing her next appearance. I left out the exact wording and most of the negotiation; I couldn’t remember it reliably, and it wasn’t worth inventing.

Next month I’d start with the previous session’s recap. If the captain came up unexpectedly, I’d search her name on the laptop and hope I’d used it consistently. The paper sheet would probably be in my notebook, but I wouldn’t want to depend on finding that particular margin again.

### 4. Shared notes and disputed memories

**Interviewer:** You added the promise in two places. What, if anything, would the players get to see of those notes? Tell me how you handle it when your recollection and theirs differ.

**Marek:** I’d put the fact that she promised them a favor in the group recap, along with whatever terms we’d clearly agreed on. I wouldn’t share my note about why she made the promise or what she might do if her employer found out. I also wouldn’t automatically share my question mark about whether she intends to keep it—that’s something I’m still deciding.

If a player remembered the promise differently, I’d ask what they recall and listen before treating my notes as the official version. The exact wording wasn’t recorded, so I wouldn’t pretend it was. If everyone agrees on a version, I’d update the recap and my notes. If we disagree, I’d try to settle on something fair before it matters in play, rather than springing my interpretation on them later.

Sometimes we leave a detail open until the captain appears again. I’d note that it’s unresolved, so next month I don’t mistake my guess for something we all agreed happened.

### 5. Editing, history, and deletion

**Interviewer:** Suppose a shared recap is changed after that disagreement. What would you expect each person to be allowed to change, and how would you want to know what happened? Are there cases where you would prefer no record?

**Marek:** I’d want players to be able to suggest corrections and add their own recollections. I’d be comfortable with them fixing an obvious typo directly, but for something like the terms of the captain’s promise, I’d rather we agree before the shared recap presents one version as settled. I’d want to be able to edit it too, with the same expectation that I don’t quietly rewrite a disputed point.

A small note like “Marek changed the promise after discussion” would be enough most of the time. For a consequential disagreement, I’d want to see the previous wording and the new wording. I don’t need a notification every time someone fixes a comma.

I wouldn’t want a permanent record of every half-formed private thought or a sensitive personal detail someone accidentally included. If a player asks me to remove something personal, I’d want it genuinely removable, not just hidden behind a history button. We might still keep a brief note that the recap was corrected, without preserving the sensitive text.

### 6. A low-risk first trial

**Interviewer:** Imagine trying a new tool for just your next session, without moving the whole campaign. What would you actually put into it first, what would you leave in your current tools, and what would make you abandon the trial?

**Marek:** I’d put in the previous session’s short recap, the captain’s name and promise, and a few notes about the archive. I’d separate what the players already know from my private notes about the captain. That’s enough to see whether I can find things during play without spending an evening copying the campaign.

I’d keep character sheets where they are, roll physical dice, and use paper for turn order and quick sketches. The group chat would stay our place for arranging sessions and sharing the recap. I wouldn’t ask the players to learn a new tool just for my experiment.

I’d abandon it if entering those few notes took longer than preparing normally, or if I had to hunt through several screens while the group waited. Unclear privacy would stop me even faster: if I couldn’t tell what players might see, I wouldn’t put my secrets in it. After the session, I’d also want to retrieve what I’d written in a usable form; I wouldn’t trust a tool that left my notes stranded.

### 7. Challenging the trial choice

**Interviewer:** Your first example was losing track of turns and an effect, but your trial leaves turn tracking on paper. Why choose notes first rather than fixing the problem that disrupted the session? What would make you change that choice?

**Marek:** Fair point. I chose notes because they feel like a low-risk trial: if the tool gets in my way, I can close it and carry on. Moving turn tracking into it during a fight would put it right in the middle of play, where a slow or confusing interaction would affect everyone.

But that also means my trial might never address the thing that actually tripped me up. I’d probably revise it: keep the turn order on paper, but try recording the temporary effects in the tool for one fight. That could tell me whether it helps, though splitting those two things might create the same problem in a new place.

I’d move turn order over too if adding combatants and adjusting the order were at least as quick as crossing things out on paper, and if I could see when an effect started without digging through notes. I’d still keep paper nearby for the first few sessions.

### 8. Giving room to disagree

**Interviewer:** I may have pushed you toward my interpretation there. You don't have to revise your trial to match my question. If you were choosing on your own, would you actually try effects during that first session, or stick with the low-risk notes trial? What matters most to that decision?

**Marek:** On my own, I’d stick with the notes trial for the first session. I know the fight exposed a real problem, but I don’t want to experiment with a new procedure while four people are waiting for me to run their turns. Paper is imperfect, but I know how to recover when I make a mess of it.

What matters most is whether I can try the tool without committing the group to it. If the notes are awkward, I can fall back on my document quietly. If effect tracking is awkward, everyone feels the interruption.

I’d still try effect tracking eventually, perhaps by entering an example before the session or during a quieter moment. If that felt quick and obvious, I might use it in a later fight. For the first session, though, I’d rather test one thing and leave the rest alone.

### 9. What the interview missed

**Interviewer:** What important part of running a session have my questions missed? Is there anything about what you want that a product team might easily misunderstand?

**Marek:** The social side. I’m not just tracking a game state; I’m watching whether someone has gone quiet, whether a scene is dragging, or whether the group needs a break. Sometimes the right thing to do is ignore the notes and follow what the players are excited about.

A product team might hear “keep the session moving” and assume I want everything faster or more automated. I don’t. A slow conversation between characters can be the best part of the evening. What I want to avoid is slowing down because I can’t find a name or remember what we agreed.

They might also mistake my messy notes for a problem that needs eliminating. Sometimes a question mark is useful: it reminds me I haven’t decided something yet. I’d be frustrated by a tool that pushes me to turn every uncertainty into a definite fact before I’m ready.

## Interviewer interpretation — hypotheses only

- Bringing relevant information together may matter more than replacing every existing tool.
- Rough notes can carry meaningful uncertainty; structured records should not automatically turn guesses into established facts.
- A group may distinguish the MG's application permissions from authority over what everyone agrees happened. Marek's suggested shared editing is not the current product permission specification.
- Useful change history and genuine removal of sensitive text can pull in opposite directions; this interview does not establish a retention policy.
- The most disruptive problem may not be the first workflow someone is willing to move into an unfamiliar tool.
- Preserving attention for people is not the same as minimizing every pause or automating every action.

**Method limitation:** Question 7 challenged the persona using the interviewer's problem framing and elicited a changed trial plan. Question 8 explicitly allowed disagreement, after which the persona returned to notes-first adoption. Both exchanges are retained rather than smoothing away the inconsistency. This illustrates sensitivity to prompting in this simulation, not an observed participant behavior.

## Questions for real participant research

- When information becomes relevant unexpectedly, how do MGs retrieve and gather it during play?
- What do rough marks, unresolved details, and duplicated notes mean in actual workflows?
- How do groups resolve different recollections and decide who may change shared records?
- Which information needs privacy, change history, or genuine deletion, and why?
- What would MGs trial first, what fallback would they keep, and how does that differ from their largest existing frustration?
- How do these patterns vary across RPG systems, devices, and remote or in-person sessions? This focused interview did not explore those differences in depth.
